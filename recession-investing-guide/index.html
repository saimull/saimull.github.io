INVESTING GUIDE

Recession psychology.
A plan for your money.

How to think about stocks, bonds, cash, property, gold, and crypto when the economy feels uncertain.

Sai Builds Wealth · Updated November 1, 2026 · About 12 minutes

Start here: Protect money you may need soon. Give the rest a clear purpose. Choose an investment mix whose losses you can afford to withstand.

The central idea: protect money you may need soon, then invest long term money in a mix whose losses you can withstand. No allocation guarantees a profit or prevents losses. [1, 2]

Understand the psychological conflict

Lower prices can look attractive before a decline and frightening during one. A falling portfolio and a possible job loss create different pressures: fear of investment losses and a real need to preserve spending money. Treat those as separate problems.

Possible trapHow it can show upA practical response
Loss aversionYou sell mainly to stop the discomfort of seeing a loss.Check whether your goal or ability to take risk actually changed.
Recency biasYou assume the latest decline will continue indefinitely.Test several outcomes instead of extending the latest trend.
Herd behaviorYou copy the asset everyone is promoting.Write down its purpose, risks, and maximum allocation first.
False certaintyYou wait for a recession announcement or a perfect bottom.Choose contribution rules that do not depend on predicting dates.

These are possible explanations for behavior, not a diagnosis. Selling can be sensible when your income, obligations, or investment timeline changes. Staying invested at any cost is not a sound rule.

Start with your ability to absorb loss

Ask two questions: How much decline could I emotionally tolerate? How much could I financially afford? Use the more restrictive answer. Someone comfortable with volatility can still be taking too much risk if next month’s mortgage depends on selling investments.

1 Build your financial foundation

Separate three types of money

Emergency reserves cover disrupted income and unexpected bills. Goal savings cover known spending such as a down payment or tuition. Long term investments fund goals that can tolerate market fluctuations. Do not count the same dollars in more than one category.

Calculate a reserve target

Reserve target = essential monthly spending × chosen months of coverage + specific one time contingencies.

Illustration: $4,000 of essential monthly spending × 6 months = $24,000. Adding a separate $3,000 contingency produces a $27,000 target. If accessible savings are $15,000, the gap is $12,000. These amounts and six months are planning assumptions, not a universal requirement.

Choose coverage based on income stability, dependents, insurance deductibles, debt payments, and how quickly you could replace income. For a household dependent on employer sponsored immigration status, also estimate relocation and transition costs; this guide does not determine immigration deadlines. Emergency savings help avoid turning an unexpected expense into debt. [3]

Match access to the expense

Keep immediately needed money accessible in an insured bank or credit union account, within applicable coverage limits. CDs can impose early withdrawal penalties. Treasury bills can serve dated needs, but check maturity and access rules. Securities held in TreasuryDirect require transfer to a bank or broker for an early sale, and transfer restrictions apply. [4, 5]

Review debt and existing commitments

High interest debt deserves attention before adding speculative investments. Interest savings from repayment are more predictable than uncertain market returns. Keep adequate liquidity rather than using every available dollar for debt repayment. [6]

List mortgage payments, other loans, property repairs, vacancies, and family obligations. For landlords, maintain a property reserve separately from household emergency money. Avoid assuming rent will always arrive on time.

View the entire balance sheet

Record property values and associated debts separately, alongside cash and investments. Home equity is not immediately spendable cash. A house in the US and a property in India add geographic exposure, but both still carry property risk. For a US household, an Indian property also introduces currency exposure.

This is a planning inference: another rental may increase your dependence on property and borrowing rather than improve your ability to handle a downturn. Concentration can also come from employer stock or overlapping funds. [7]

2 Understand what each asset does

An asset class is a type of investment. An ETF is a wrapper that can hold stocks, bonds, or other exposures. Buying five ETFs does not necessarily mean owning five different sources of risk. Check their actual holdings. [7]

Cash and short term billsSpending flexibility and dated expensesInflation; reinvestment at lower rates; access restrictions depending on product.
Broad US stock fundsLong term participation in company growthLarge losses; US market and sector concentration.
Broad international stock fundsExposure to companies outside the USMarket, currency, and political risk; can fall with US stocks.
High quality bondsIncome and a possible balance to stock volatilityInterest rate and credit risk. Longer maturity funds can be more sensitive to rate changes. [8]
Real estateRental income or property exposureVacancy, repairs, leverage, transaction costs, and slow sales. REITs differ from directly owned property.
GoldOptional exposure to a different price driverNo interest or dividends from the metal itself; volatile prices, fees or storage costs.
Crypto assetsOptional speculative exposureRapid price changes and custody risks; unsuitable as a substitute for emergency reserves. [9]

Choose by purpose before choosing a product

First write the job: for example, “money for a purchase in two years” or “retirement spending decades from now.” Then decide how much market risk that job permits. Only then compare funds or accounts, including their costs and access terms.

Stocks, bonds, and cash can form a complete starting structure. You do not need gold, crypto, or a rental simply to claim diversification. Adding assets creates value only if their role and risks fit your plan. [1, 2]

Distinguish bonds from bond funds

A bond held to maturity has a stated repayment date, subject to issuer repayment. A typical bond fund has a changing portfolio and no date when your original purchase amount is guaranteed back. Treasury bond funds can also lose value as interest rates change. [8, 10]

3 Test a portfolio before using it

The following mix demonstrates arithmetic only. It is not a recommendation, an optimized portfolio, or a recession forecast. Emergency reserves and near term goal savings are outside this example.

Investment categoryTargetOf $10,000Of $500 monthly
Broad US stocks40%$4,000$200
Broad international stocks20%$2,000$100
High quality bonds30%$3,000$150
Cash or short term bills10%$1,000$50
Total100%$10,000$500

Run a hypothetical loss scenario

Assume both stock categories fall 35%, bonds fall 10%, and cash has a 0% return. These are invented stress assumptions, not historical results or estimates of what will happen.

Portfolio change = (60% × −35%) + (30% × −10%) + (10% × 0%) = −24%. A $10,000 portfolio becomes $7,600, before contributions, fees, and taxes.

This is the strongest counterargument to calling a diversified portfolio “safe”: several holdings can decline together. Diversification manages exposure; it cannot eliminate market losses. [2]

Combine market risk with income risk

Now assume income stops for six months while your portfolio is down 24%. Can reserves cover essential spending without selling investments? What if a rental is vacant or your car needs a repair at the same time? Use your actual bills and include one time costs.

If the answer is no, consider building reserves, reducing obligations, or choosing less investment risk. A high expected return does not repair a cash shortage when bills are due.

Check recovery math

After a 24% loss, a 24% gain does not restore the starting balance. $7,600 × 1.24 = $9,424. Returning to $10,000 requires approximately 31.6%, with no withdrawals or additions. This calculation says nothing about how quickly recovery will occur.

Check your own mix

Use: total scenario return = sum of each asset’s weight × its assumed return. Test both a growth shock and an inflation or interest rate shock. Do not assume bonds or gold must rise whenever stocks fall.

4 Write rules you can follow

Put the plan into practice

1. Inventory all accounts, funds, debts, property, and cash. Group holdings by underlying exposure, not account name. A retirement account is an account type, not an asset class.

2. Separate reserves and dated goals. Choose an investment mix for the remaining money. Consider your timeline, income stability, and maximum affordable loss. [1]

3. Compare products for broad exposure, fees, overlap, credit quality, and bond duration. Read the product information before buying. A narrow sector ETF may add concentration. [7, 8]

4. Automate a contribution you can afford. Regular investing reduces dependence on a single entry date, but does not guarantee profit or protect against losses. Reduce or pause contributions if essential spending or reserves require it. [6, 11]

5. Review on a schedule. One workable rule is a review every 6 or 12 months. Rebalance if appropriate by directing new contributions toward underweight assets; consider taxes and costs before selling. Review sooner after a material life change. [1, 12]

Complete your investment policy

Write down these answers in your own notes before choosing your mix:

  • Your goal and when you expect to need the money.
  • Essential monthly spending, your reserve target, and accessible reserves today.
  • Separate savings for planned purchases and rental property expenses.
  • Your target percentages for stocks, bonds, cash, and any other assets, totaling 100%.
  • An affordable monthly contribution and a scheduled review date.
  • A loss scenario combined with an income interruption.
  • What you would change if the plan became unaffordable.

No financial details are collected on this page.

Evidence and limits

Diversification can reduce concentration risk. It cannot guarantee protection in a downturn. Your current balances, debts, goals, reserve needs, and investment timeline are needed before choosing personal percentages.
