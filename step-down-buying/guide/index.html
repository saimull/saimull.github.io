The beginner guide

Step-down buying.
Made simple.

Step-down buying means starting with a small investment and buying a little more when the price falls by a set amount.

You decide your buying plan before you start, so you do not have to guess what to do every time the price drops.

How it works

  • Set your total budget. Decide the most you are willing to invest.
  • Start small. Invest a small part of that budget first.
  • Choose your price drops. For example, plan another purchase after each 5% drop from the previous buying level.
  • Increase purchases gradually. You could start with $100, then buy $120, then $140.
  • Stop at your limit. Do not keep adding money beyond your budget.

A simple example

Suppose a stock starts at $160. You plan four purchases, increasing each purchase by $20.

PurchaseStock priceAmount you invest
First$160.00$100
Second$152.00$120
Third$144.40$140
Fourth$137.18$160

Each price is 5% below the previous level. Your total planned investment is $520.

If all four purchases happen at these prices, your average cost becomes about $146.46 per share.

That means the stock would need to recover to about $146.46 for you to break even before fees and taxes. It would not need to return to $160.

What if the price goes up?

You still own your first purchase, but the remaining money stays available. You may miss some gains because you invested only a small amount initially.

A few rules to remember

  • A lower price does not automatically mean a good investment. Check why it fell before buying more.
  • Buy once at each planned level. Repeated visits to the same price do not mean repeated purchases.
  • If the price suddenly skips several levels, pause and review. Do not rush to make every purchase at once.
  • Use money you can afford to leave invested. Keep emergency savings separate and avoid borrowing to buy more.
  • Stop if your reason for investing no longer holds. You do not have to finish the plan.

The goal is to spread out your purchases and lower your average cost as you buy at lower prices. You can still lose money if the investment keeps falling or never recovers.

Illustrative example only. The amounts and 5% interval are examples, not a recommendation.
