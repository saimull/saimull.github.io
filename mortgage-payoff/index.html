Mortgage payoff checklist

A paid off home can give you more breathing room. Getting there should still leave money for everyday life, repairs, and your future. Use this checklist to choose a plan you can afford and understand what you give up.

Read the tradeoffs, then check each item as you review it. Checks stay on this page until you reload it.

1. Biweekly payments

Pay half your monthly principal and interest every two weeks. In a typical year, 26 half payments equal 13 full payments. Keep taxes and insurance funded as required by your servicer.

Pros: Can fit a biweekly paycheck and adds one extra payment each year.

Cons: Some programs charge fees or hold partial payments. Paying twice a month only creates 24 half payments and does not add an extra payment.

2. One extra payment a year

Divide your monthly principal and interest payment by 12. Add that amount to principal each month. A $1,800 payment means an extra $150 each month.

Pros: Easy to automate without enrolling in a biweekly program.

Cons: You still need room in your budget. A payment made at year end saves less interest than the same money paid earlier.

3. Extra principal every month

Pick an amount you can keep paying, whether that is $100, $250, or $500. Send it with your regular payment using the principal option.

Pros: Reduces the balance earlier and usually lets you pause the extra amount when life gets expensive.

Cons: The cash becomes home equity. You generally need to sell or qualify for new borrowing to access it again.

4. Round up your payment

If your total payment is $1,865, pay $2,000 and direct the extra $135 to principal.

Pros: Simple to remember and easy to start with a small amount.

Cons: The effect may be modest. Escrow increases can shrink the extra amount if you keep paying the same total.

5. Use part of a windfall

Choose a share of bonuses, tax refunds, or other unexpected money for principal after setting aside taxes and near term needs.

Pros: Makes progress without raising your regular monthly commitment.

Cons: Windfalls are unpredictable. Sending the whole amount can leave you short when a repair or other expense comes up.

6. Put part of each raise toward principal

When your take home pay increases, send part of the increase to the mortgage. For example, put $150 of a $300 monthly raise toward principal.

Pros: Builds a larger payment gradually without cutting your current spending by the full amount.

Cons: Other costs may rise too. Using every raise for the mortgage can crowd out retirement savings and other goals.

7. Pay on a shorter schedule

Use your current balance and interest rate to calculate the payment needed to finish in 15 or 20 years. Pay the difference as extra principal.

Pros: Keeps your existing loan and its lower required payment. No refinance is needed to pay faster.

Cons: You keep your existing interest rate and need discipline. The faster schedule can require a large monthly increase.

8. Refinance when the numbers work

Compare your current loan with written refinance offers. Include closing costs, any costs added to the balance, interest, and the payoff date.

Pros: A better rate or shorter term can reduce future interest.

Cons: Fees can erase savings. Restarting a 30 year term can extend the debt, and a shorter term can raise your required payment.

9. Understand a mortgage recast

After a substantial principal payment, an eligible lender may recalculate your payment using the lower balance and remaining term. The rate stays the same.

Pros: Can lower your required payment while keeping your existing rate.

Cons: May require a fee and minimum lump sum. Not all loans qualify. Paying only the new minimum keeps the remaining schedule instead of accelerating payoff.

10. Be skeptical of HELOC payoff pitches

A HELOC moves debt into a line of credit secured by your home. Compare it with sending the same extra cash directly to your mortgage.

Pros: A truly lower borrowing cost could help in a specific case, and a credit line may offer flexibility.

Cons: Rates usually vary. Fees, repeated borrowing, and a higher rate can wipe out savings. Your home secures the debt and missed payments can put it at risk.

11. Know when extra payments should wait

Before paying extra, review emergency savings, expensive debt, employer retirement matches, and upcoming expenses.

Pros: Keeps cash available and may let you address a more expensive problem first.

Cons: Your mortgage stays around longer and you may pay more mortgage interest. Keeping cash only helps if you use it deliberately.

12. Choose a plan you can keep doing

Pick one regular payment method and an optional windfall rule. Revisit the plan when income, expenses, or priorities change.

Pros: Makes progress easier to track and keeps the payment realistic.

Cons: A smaller payment may mean a later payoff than you hoped. Automation still needs occasional review.

Before I send extra money

Write down your plan

Choose your base strategy, monthly extra amount, share of windfalls, and start date. Set a date to review the plan. The PDF includes space to fill these in.

If paying extra means borrowing for ordinary bills or skipping necessary savings, lower or pause the extra amount.

Property taxes, insurance, maintenance, and any HOA dues continue after the mortgage is paid off.
